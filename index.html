The Problem Nobody Wants to Admit

Most Product Ads Are Visually Dead Before They're Published

Let me be brutally honest with you. I've reviewed thousands of ads over the years. Written copy for brands you know and brands you haven't heard of yet. And one thing has stayed painfully consistent: brands spend almost all of their creative energy on the wrong thing.

They labor over the headline. They agonize over the CTA button color. They A/B test subject lines down to the individual word. And then — without a second thought — they slap a stock photo or a bland white-background product shot next to all of that beautifully crafted copy.

And the ad dies. Not because the copy was bad. Because the image gave nobody a reason to stop scrolling.

"Nobody stops for a product shot. People stop for a story. And the image has to tell the first chapter of that story before a single word gets read." — Ken Moskowitz, Ad Zombies

The visual is the hook. The copy is the close. If the hook doesn't land, no amount of brilliant copy in the world will save you. Your ad might as well not exist.

But here's what's changed — and why this is actually good news: you no longer need a $5,000 photo shoot to create scroll-stopping product images. You need the right AI tools, the right prompts, and an understanding of what visual storytelling actually looks like in the context of an ad.

The Death March

The Way Brands Used to Do This (And Why It's Broken)

I want to walk you through the traditional product photography process — not to trash it, but to show you exactly where the money goes, where the time disappears, and why the whole system is designed to produce mediocrity at scale.

  1. Hire a photographer

    $1,800–$6,000 per day for a food or product photographer worth working with. Day rates don't include editing, retouching, or usage rights. Those are extra.

  2. Book a studio or location

    Half-day studio rental: $400–$1,200. Full day: double that. On-location shoots add travel, location fees, and the beautiful chaos of things that weren't in the shot list.

  3. Hire a food stylist (if you're smart)

    $800–$2,500 per day. Because raw product out of the bag never looks like what you need. Stylist fees are non-negotiable if you want the image to actually perform.

  4. Wait 10–21 days for edited selects

    Good photographers are busy photographers. You'll wait. You'll follow up. You'll wait some more. And when the images arrive, you'll realize you only got 8 usable selects out of a 400-image shoot.

  5. Test one or two versions

    You spent $10,000 to end up with two viable ad creatives. You run them. One performs okay. One flops. You have no idea why — and you can't afford to go back to find out.

  6. Repeat the entire cycle in 90 days

    Because the market moved. The trend changed. The audience got used to the visual. And you're back at square one with the same budget and the same constraints.

Total spend for one product photo shoot: $8,000–$15,000+
Usable images: 6–12
Variations for testing: Maybe 2–3
Time to market: 3–5 weeks
Flexibility to iterate: None. You get what you paid for.

This isn't a workflow. It's a financial commitment to mediocrity dressed up in expensive lighting. And the brands doing it this way aren't just spending more — they're testing less, learning slower, and leaving money on the table every single day.

The Playbook

How to Generate Product Images That Actually Sell: Step by Step

Alright. You're convinced. You want to try this. Here's exactly how we approach it — the same process we use when we're building out ad creative for a product brand from scratch.

  1. Start with the emotional target, not the product

    Before you write a single prompt, ask yourself: what do I want the viewer to FEEL when they see this image? Hungry? Nostalgic? Excited? Comforted? The answer to that question drives everything that comes after. A Thai peanut noodle dish should make someone feel warm, satisfied, maybe a little adventurous. Build that feeling into the prompt.

  2. Choose your visual context intentionally

    Where is the product being used or consumed? By whom? In what setting? The context tells the audience who the product is for. A cozy kitchen says family. A sleek marble countertop says premium. A camping table says adventure. Pick the context that matches your buyer persona and hold to it.

  3. Write a detailed, specific prompt

    Vague prompts produce vague images. The more specific you are — lighting direction, surface texture, camera angle, mood, garnishes, background elements — the more control you have over the output. Think of it the way you'd brief a food photographer: every detail is intentional.

  4. Generate 10–20 variations

    Don't stop at one. Generate a volume of options. Adjust the prompt between rounds — change the lighting, shift the angle, try a different surface. You're looking for the two or three images that make you stop and actually feel something. You'll know them when you see them.

  5. Select based on scroll-stopping potential, not personal preference

    The question isn't "which one do I like best?" The question is "which one would stop me if I was mindlessly scrolling at 11pm?" Those are different answers. Be ruthless. Your opinion doesn't buy products — your customers' attention does.

  6. Write copy that completes the visual story

    Now — and only now — do you write the headline. The image already said something. The copy finishes the thought. If the image made them hungry, the headline deepens the craving. If the image made them curious, the headline delivers the payoff. The two elements work together or they work against each other. Make them work together.

  7. Test fast, learn faster, scale what wins

    Run three to five image variations simultaneously with a small budget. Let the data tell you which one is winning within 48–72 hours. Kill the losers. Feed the winner. Rinse and repeat with fresh variations to keep the winning creative from fatiguing.

Where Copy Meets Creative

The Image Gets Them to Stop. The Copy Gets Them to Buy.

Here's something most marketers miss — and it's the thing that separates a good ad from a great one. The image and the copy aren't two separate decisions. They're one conversation. The image starts the sentence. The copy finishes it.

Look at how the emotional framing of the image should directly inform the copy approach:

01

Comfort Image → Nostalgia Copy

If the image says "warm Sunday evening at home," the headline should say "the dinner you didn't have to think about." Match the feeling, don't redirect it.

02

Premium Image → Aspiration Copy

Dark, moody, elevated visual? Your copy should lean into identity, not price. "This is what dinner looks like when you actually care about the experience."

03

Lifestyle Image → Belonging Copy

Woman laughing in the kitchen? The copy speaks to the person who wants to feel like that. Relatable, aspirational, and just specific enough to feel personal.

04

Ingredient Image → Credibility Copy

All those beautiful raw ingredients? The copy earns trust. "Real ingredients. Real flavors. Ready in eight minutes." The image proved it. The copy names it.

What to Avoid

The Mistakes That Turn AI Product Images Into Expensive Garbage

I'd be doing you a disservice if I made this all sound effortless. There's a right way to do this and a deeply wrong way to do it. The wrong way is more common than you'd think. Here's what to watch for.

!

Vague, lazy prompts

"A picture of my product looking nice" produces exactly the kind of generic, forgettable image you're trying to escape. Specificity is everything. Write the brief like you'd write a creative brief for a million-dollar campaign.

!

Ignoring hands and faces

AI still has a complicated relationship with human hands and expressions. If you're prompting lifestyle images with people, review carefully. Nothing kills credibility like an extra finger on the product or a smile that looks slightly… wrong.

!

Mismatching image mood and copy tone

Moody, dark, premium image paired with breezy, casual copy creates cognitive dissonance. The buyer's brain gets confused. Confusion kills conversion. Make sure the emotional voice of the copy matches the emotional voice of the image.

!

Stopping at one generation

The first result is rarely the best result. Generate multiples. Compare. Iterate. The image that converts is almost never the first one the AI produces — it's the one you get after you've refined the prompt three or four times.

    The creative testing loop is never finished. The best-performing image you've ever produced is just the baseline for the next round of testing.
